## SCENE 2: PUNCTUALITY — "TIME IS RESPECT"

*[Clock on wall reads 8:52 AM. Aiko walks through the front door, uniform pressed, hair neatly tied back. She is 8 minutes early. Kenji greets her with a warm smile.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Our first principle: **Punctuality**.

**KENJI:**
*(in Japanese, with English subtitles)*
Aiko-san, ohayou gozaimasu. Good morning. I see you are early — excellent. In Japan, arriving a few minutes before your shift begins is a sign of respect for your colleagues, your managers, and your customers.

**AIKO:**
I wanted to make sure I had time to prepare before we open.

**KENJI:**
Exactly. Being late — even by a few minutes — can suggest carelessness. Here at Shining Star, we consider arriving *on time* to actually mean arriving *late*.

*[On-screen text appears:]*
> ⏰ **Punctuality Tip:** Aim to arrive 5–10 minutes before your shift. Notify your supervisor immediately if an emergency makes you late.

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
This principle extends to our business clients as well. If a corporate customer schedules a vehicle fleet cleaning at a specific time, our team must be fully prepared and ready *before* that time arrives. Punctuality communicates professionalism and reliability.

## SCENE 3: GREETINGS AND BOWING — "THE BOW SPEAKS VOLUMES"

*[The front entrance. A well-dressed business customer, Mr. Tanaka, approaches the reception desk.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
In Japan, the bow is more than a gesture — it is a language of its own. Let's watch how Kenji greets a business client.

*[Mr. Tanaka enters. Kenji immediately stops what he is doing, turns to face Mr. Tanaka, and bows at approximately 30 degrees.]*

**KENJI:**
*(warmly, with a bow)*
Irasshaimase! Welcome to Shining Star, Tanaka-san. We are honored to have you with us today.

*[Mr. Tanaka returns the bow.]*

**MR. TANAKA:**
Thank you, Kenji-san.

*[Kenji turns to Aiko and quietly explains.]*

**KENJI:**
*(to Aiko, quietly)*
Did you notice the angle of my bow? A slight bow of about 15 degrees is a casual, polite greeting. A deeper bow — around 30 degrees — shows greater respect, which is appropriate for a valued business client. For the deepest sign of apology or reverence, we bow even further — about 45 degrees or more.

**AIKO:**
How do I know when to use which?

**KENJI:**
As a general guide at work: greet all customers with at least a 15-degree bow, and our corporate or long-standing clients with a 30-degree bow. You will develop a feel for it over time.

*[On-screen graphic shows three bow angles with labels:]*
> 🙇 **15°** — Casual, polite greeting
> 🙇 **30°** — Respectful greeting for clients and seniors
> 🙇 **45°** — Deep apology or reverence

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
A few additional notes on greetings:
- Always **stop what you are doing** and give the customer your full attention when greeting them.
- Use the customer's **last name with the honorific "-san"** unless they invite you to do otherwise.
- If a foreign business customer extends a handshake, accept it warmly — but let them initiate it.

## SCENE 4: BUSINESS CARDS (MEISHI) — "THE CARD IS THE PERSON"

*[Reception desk. Mr. Tanaka reaches into his jacket and presents a business card to Aiko. She hesitates, unsure what to do. Kenji steps in.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
For employees who manage business accounts or interact with corporate clients, understanding business card etiquette — known as *meishi koukan* — is essential.

*[Kenji gently guides Aiko through the process.]*

**KENJI:**
*(quietly, to Aiko)*
Watch carefully.

*[Kenji receives Mr. Tanaka's card with both hands, holding it gently at the corners. He bows slightly, reads the card carefully, and says:]*

**KENJI:**
Tanaka-san, Fleet Manager at Yamamoto Logistics — thank you very much.

*[He places the card respectfully on the desk in front of him, face up.]*

**KENJI:**
*(to Aiko)*
The business card represents the person. You must receive it with both hands, take a moment to actually *read* it, and treat it with care. Never write on it, fold it, or shove it in your pocket.

**AIKO:**
What if I receive several cards during a meeting?

**KENJI:**
Lay them out on the table in front of you in the order you received them — it helps you remember names and shows respect. After the meeting, store them carefully in a card holder.

*[On-screen text:]*
> 📇 **Meishi Etiquette Summary:**
> ✅ Receive and present with both hands
> ✅ Bow slightly upon receiving
> ✅ Read the card carefully
> ✅ Place it respectfully on the table during meetings
> ❌ Never write on, fold, or casually pocket a card

## SCENE 5: HIERARCHY AND RESPECT — "KNOW YOUR PLACE IN THE CIRCLE"

*[Break room. Aiko and Kenji sit down for a brief orientation. Other team members are present.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Japanese business culture places significant importance on **hierarchy and respect**. Understanding the social structure of your workplace — and of your clients' organizations — will help you navigate interactions with confidence.

**KENJI:**
At Shining Star, we respect seniority. When a new business client visits — especially if they bring a team — always acknowledge the most senior person first. They are usually the one who enters first or stands at the center.

**AIKO:**
How can I tell who is the most senior?

**KENJI:**
Observe the group dynamic. Others may defer to that person — stepping back, listening more, letting them speak first. Their business card will also tell you their title. When in doubt, acknowledge the eldest-looking individual first.

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
This also applies to our team structure. Here at Shining Star, we follow a clear chain of respect:

*[On-screen org chart appears showing: Manager → Senior Staff → Staff → Part-time Employees]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
When addressing a senior colleague, use their name with the appropriate honorific. For example, "Kenji-san" or, if they hold a managerial position, "Kenji-kacho" — *kacho* meaning section chief or manager. Always wait for senior staff to be seated first at team meetings, and allow them to speak before offering your own opinion.

*[Kenji demonstrates entering a meeting room, gesturing for a "senior guest" to take the seat farthest from the door — known as the seat of honor, or **kamiza**.]*

**KENJI:**
The seat farthest from the door is the *kamiza* — the seat of honor. Always guide your most important guest or senior colleague there. The seat closest to the door is the *shimoza* — typically where the most junior person sits.

*[On-screen diagram of a meeting table with labeled seating positions.]*

## SCENE 6: COMMUNICATION — "WHAT IS NOT SAID MATTERS TOO"

*[Simulated customer service scenario. Aiko is speaking with Mr. Tanaka about upgrading his company's car wash package.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
In Japan, communication is often **indirect**. Silence, subtle hesitation, and gentle deflection can all carry meaning. Learning to read between the lines is a critical skill.

**MR. TANAKA:**
*(thoughtfully)*
Hmm... the premium package is very interesting. We will... consider it.

*[Aiko looks uncertain. She pulls Kenji aside afterward.]*

**AIKO:**
He said he would consider it. Does that mean yes?

**KENJI:**
Not necessarily. In Japanese business culture, a direct "no" is often avoided to preserve harmony. When someone says "we will consider it," "it may be difficult," or "that is an interesting idea" — these phrases can often mean "no," or at least signal strong hesitation.

**AIKO:**
So how should I respond?

**KENJI:**
Do not push for an immediate answer. Thank him sincerely, provide printed materials for him to review, and follow up politely at a later time. Patience is seen as maturity.

*[On-screen graphic:]*
> 💬 **Indirect Language Guide:**
> - *"That may be difficult"* → Likely means NO
> - *"We will consider it"* → Uncertain / leaning toward NO
> - *"It is very interesting"* → Could mean NO without commitment
> - Silence / slight intake of breath → Discomfort or disagreement

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Additional communication guidelines at Shining Star:
- **Never interrupt** a customer or senior colleague while they are speaking.
- **Avoid confrontation** — if a customer is unhappy, remain calm, bow, and apologize before offering solutions.
- **Do not raise your voice** under any circumstances on the floor.
- Silence is not awkward — it is **comfortable and meaningful**. Do not rush to fill every pause.

## SCENE 7: CONFLICT RESOLUTION AND "FACE-SAVING"

*[Service area. A customer is displeased that their car was not ready at the promised time. Aiko approaches.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
One of the most important concepts in Japanese business culture is **"face" — or *mentsu***. This refers to a person's reputation, dignity, and social standing. In all interactions, it is critical that you protect both your customer's face and your own company's face.

*[Customer looks visibly frustrated.]*

**CUSTOMER:**
I was told my car would be ready by 2:00. It is now 2:20.

*[Aiko bows deeply — approximately 45 degrees — and responds calmly.]*

**AIKO:**
*(sincerely)*
Tanaka-san, I sincerely apologize for this delay. We deeply regret the inconvenience caused. Please allow me to check on the status immediately and ensure this is resolved for you right away.

*[Aiko steps away, updates the customer within two minutes, and the car is delivered with a complimentary air freshener as an apology gesture.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Notice what Aiko did:
- She **bowed deeply**, acknowledging the seriousness of the error
- She **did not make excuses** or blame colleagues
- She **took ownership** and acted swiftly
- She **offered a small gesture of goodwill**

*[On-screen text:]*
> 🎌 **Face-Saving Principles:**
> ✅ Accept responsibility gracefully
> ✅ Never publicly blame a colleague
> ✅ Apologize sincerely and bow appropriately
> ✅ Resolve the issue promptly
> ❌ Never argue with a customer in front of others

## SCENE 8: GIFT-GIVING — "THE ART OF THE GESTURE"

*[Reception area. Kenji is preparing a small wrapped gift for a long-standing corporate client who is visiting for an annual contract renewal.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Gift-giving, or *omiyage*, is a meaningful tradition in Japanese business culture. At Shining Star, we practice thoughtful gift-giving for our valued corporate clients on special occasions — such as the New Year, contract renewals, or milestone anniversaries.

**KENJI:**
*(to Aiko)*
We are renewing our contract with Yamamoto Logistics today. I have prepared a small gift — some quality Japanese sweets — to express our gratitude for their continued trust.

**AIKO:**
Should I mention the gift right away?

**KENJI:**
No. Present it humbly, near the end of the meeting. Use two hands, bow slightly, and say something like: *"This is a small token of our appreciation — it is nothing special, but we hope you enjoy it."* This modesty is important, even if the gift is very thoughtful.

**AIKO:**
Will they open it in front of us?

**KENJI:**
Probably not. It is common in Japan to open gifts later, in private. Do not feel offended by this — it is simply good etiquette.

*[On-screen tips:]*
> 🎁 **Gift-Giving Guidelines:**
> ✅ Wrap gifts carefully and beautifully
> ✅ Present with both hands and a bow
> ✅ Use humble language when presenting
> ✅ Appropriate gifts: quality food items, local specialties, premium consumables
> ❌ Avoid sets of 4 (associated with death — *shi*)
> ❌ Avoid sets of 9 (associated with suffering — *ku*)
> ❌ Do not expect the gift to be opened immediately

## SCENE 9: BUILDING RELATIONSHIPS — "BUSINESS IS PERSONAL"

*[After-hours. The Shining Star team gathers for a small team dinner to welcome Aiko and celebrate a successful quarter.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
In Japan, business is deeply rooted in **relationships built over time**. Trust is not assumed — it is earned through consistency, reliability, and genuine human connection. This applies both to your teammates and to your clients.

**KENJI:**
*(raising a glass of tea)*
In Japanese culture, we often pour for others before pouring for ourselves. Aiko-san — allow me.

*[Kenji pours tea for Aiko and the others before pouring his own.]*

**KENJI:**
This small act says: *I see you. I value you. Your comfort comes before mine.*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
At Shining Star, we nurture long-term relationships with our corporate clients through:
- **Consistent, high-quality service** every single time
- **Remembering preferences** — how they like their vehicles cleaned, their scheduling preferences, their names and titles
- **Following up** after service with a polite check-in
- **Seasonal greetings** — a simple card at New Year or midsummer goes a long way

*[On-screen text:]*
> 🤝 **Relationship-Building at Shining Star:**
> - Remember client names, titles, and preferences
> - Follow up after corporate service visits
> - Send seasonal greeting cards (*nengajo* for New Year)
> - Be consistent — trust is built through repetition

## SCENE 10: DRESS CODE AND PROFESSIONAL APPEARANCE

*[Locker room / uniform area. Kenji reviews the dress code with Aiko.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Your appearance is a direct reflection of Shining Star's professionalism. In Japan, **conservative, clean, and neat presentation** is expected in any business setting.

**KENJI:**
Your uniform must always be clean and pressed before your shift. Hair should be neatly tied back or kept tidy. Minimal jewelry. Clean shoes. No strong perfume or cologne — some customers are sensitive.

**AIKO:**
What about on days when we have corporate client visits?

**KENJI:**
On those days, we wear our formal uniform and take extra care with presentation. If you are unsure, it is always better to be slightly overdressed than underdressed.

*[On-screen checklist:]*
> 👔 **Daily Uniform Standards:**
> ✅ Clean, pressed uniform
> ✅ Name badge worn at all times
> ✅ Hair neat and tidy
> ✅ Minimal, appropriate accessories
> ✅ Clean, polished footwear
> ❌ No strong fragrances
> ❌ No visible tattoos (cover if present)
> ❌ No casual or torn clothing

## SCENE 11: RECAP AND CLOSING — "THE SHINING STAR PROMISE"

*[Clean, bright meeting room. Kenji and Aiko sit across from each other. Kenji closes his notebook and smiles.]*

**KENJI:**
You have covered a lot today, Aiko-san. How are you feeling?

Honestly, there is a lot to remember. But it feels... meaningful. Like each of these practices exists for a real reason.

**KENJI:**
Exactly. These are not just rules. They are expressions of respect — for our customers, our colleagues, and our culture. When a customer leaves their car with us, they are trusting us with something valuable. How we treat that trust — and how we treat *them* — is the heart of everything we do.

As a Shining Star team member, you represent not only our business — but the very best of Japanese hospitality and professionalism. Let's recap the core principles you have learned today:

Welcome to the Shining Star family. We are proud to have you with us. Carry these principles with you every day — and remember: a great car wash cleans the vehicle, but great etiquette cleans the soul of the business.
